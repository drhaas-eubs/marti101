MARTI101 · Unit 6 · Pre-Reading

Pre-reading: Risk Is Not a Property of the Technology

Key frameworks: The EU AI Act Risk Pyramid, read against the global regulatory map · Sources of Harm in the Machine Learning (ML) Lifecycle

Human-Centred Implications of AI: Ethics, Society & Regulation · Format: Business Challenge

Learning Focus · ILO 5 · integrates ILOs 1–4

Evaluate key ethical and legal considerations for business leaders adopting AI.

The same AI system can be lawful in one market, heavily regulated in a second and prohibited in a third. Nothing about the model changes. What changes is the use, the role you play and the jurisdiction.

The Storyline (after Bostelaar)

Beginning · Misbelief

"AI regulation" means the EU AI Act, and compliance is a check at the end of the project.

Middle · Insight

Every major economy regulates AI, but by a different logic. The EU Act itself reaches firms outside Europe whenever a system's output is used in the Union.

End · Understanding

A leader classifies every system on three axes before building it: what it is used for, which legal role the firm plays, and where its outputs land.

1 · Define before you classify

Precision matters because every obligation in the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) is triggered by a defined term. If a system is not an "AI system", or your firm is not its "provider", the obligation does not attach. The definitions below are paraphrased; cite the article, not this page.

AI system
A machine-based system, designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and possibly adaptive after deployment, that infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions able to influence physical or virtual environments. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) uses a near-identical definition, which is why it travels across jurisdictions.Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 3(1); OECD (2024)
Provider
Whoever develops an AI system or general-purpose AI model, or has it developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge.Art. 3(3)
Deployer
Whoever uses an AI system under its own authority, other than in a purely personal, non-professional activity. Most companies in this course are deployers, not providers. A deployer that rebrands a system, or substantially modifies it, can become its provider.Art. 3(4); Art. 25
General-purpose AI (GPAI) model
An AI model that displays significant generality, can competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks and can be integrated into many downstream systems. Large language models are the standard example. Models used only for research or prototyping before release are excluded.Art. 3(63)
High-risk AI system
Either (a) a system that is, or is a safety component of, a product covered by the EU product-safety legislation listed in Annex I and subject to third-party conformity assessment, or (b) a system used in one of the areas listed in Annex III: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, access to essential private and public services (including credit and insurance), law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice and democratic processes. An Annex III system can escape the classification if it poses no significant risk, for example when it performs only a narrow procedural task, but never if it profiles natural persons.Art. 6; Annexes I and III

Key framework 1: The EU AI Act Risk Pyramid

The Act sorts obligations by the risk of the use, not by the sophistication of the technology. The pyramid is a teaching simplification: transparency duties apply across tiers, and general-purpose AI models sit beside the pyramid with their own regime.

Unacceptable risk · prohibitedPractices such as social scoring, manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, emotion recognition in the workplace and in education, and untargeted scraping of facial images. Applies from 2 February 2025. The Digital Omnibus on AI adds two prohibitions on generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material, applying from 2 December 2026.
High risk · conformity before marketRisk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, plus registration. Deployers must use the system as instructed, assign competent human oversight and, in some cases, assess the impact on fundamental rights. After the Omnibus: Annex III systems from 2 December 2027, Annex I systems from 2 August 2028.
Limited risk · transparencyUsers must know they are interacting with AI; synthetic or manipulated content and deepfakes must be marked or disclosed (Art. 50).
Minimal risk · no new obligationsMost business AI (spam filters, inventory forecasting). Voluntary codes encouraged.
Beside the pyramid · general-purpose AI modelsDocumentation, copyright policy and a summary of training content for all providers; additional evaluation, incident-reporting and cybersecurity duties for models with systemic risk. Applies from 2 August 2025.

Cutting across every tier: since 2 February 2025, Article 4 requires providers and deployers to take measures supporting the AI literacy of their staff (see Unit 2).

Key Points

  • Classification follows the use case: the same model is minimal-risk as a writing aid and high-risk as a CV-screening tool.
  • Roles decide duties: providers carry most obligations, but deployers are never obligation-free.
  • The Act is extraterritorial: a provider in the United States or China is covered when its system's output is used in the Union.
  • Deadlines move: the 2026 Omnibus shifted the high-risk dates, so check the consolidated text before you cite a date.

2 · Read the pyramid against the global map

The EU is one regulatory model among several. Multinational firms comply with all of them at once, and their strategies often follow the strictest regime that applies to the largest market.

JurisdictionMain instrument(s)Regulatory logic
European UnionAI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; alongside the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)Horizontal, binding, risk-based; covers every sector; extraterritorial.
Council of EuropeFramework Convention on AI and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225), opened for signature 2024; signed by, among others, the EU, the United Kingdom, the United States and Japan; ratified by the EU in May 2026First binding international treaty on AI; obliges states, not firms directly; focuses on human rights.
United StatesNo federal AI statute. Executive Order 14365 (December 2025) directs federal agencies to challenge state AI laws, but does not itself preempt them. State laws: e.g. Colorado's AI Act, rewritten in 2026 before it ever took effect, now applies to consequential decisions from 1 January 2027Sectoral and state-by-state, enforced through existing consumer-protection and anti-discrimination law; federal policy favours a light touch.
ChinaInterim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (2023); rules on algorithmic recommendation (2022) and deep synthesis (2023); Measures for Labelling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content (from 1 September 2025). A comprehensive AI law is planned but not in forceService- and content-specific; registration of algorithms with the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC); strong content controls.
South KoreaAct on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of Trust ("AI Basic Act"), in force since 22 January 2026Horizontal framework; duties for "high-impact" AI and generative-AI labelling; foreign firms above thresholds need a local representative.
JapanAct on Promotion of Research, Development and Utilization of AI-Related Technologies ("AI Promotion Act"), 2025Promotion-first; cooperation duties, no monetary penalties; relies on guidelines.
United KingdomNo AI-specific statute; five cross-sector principles applied by existing regulators (2023 White Paper)Principles-based, regulator-led, pro-innovation.

Soft law and standards: the common language underneath

Firms that operate across these regimes rarely build one compliance programme per country. They anchor governance in international principles and standards that every regime recognises:

  • Principles: OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024); United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021); G7 Hiroshima Process International Code of Conduct for organisations developing advanced AI (2023).
  • Standards: International Organization for Standardization / International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) 42001 (AI management system, certifiable) and ISO/IEC 23894 (AI risk management guidance); National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework with its four functions govern, map, measure and manage.

Four regulatory logics

Risk-based, horizontal

European Union, South Korea. One law, all sectors, obligations scaled to the risk of the use.

Sectoral and fragmented

United States. Existing laws plus state statutes; obligations depend on sector and state.

Content and service control

China. Targeted rules per technology type, with registration and content requirements.

Promotion-first, principles-led

Japan, United Kingdom. Few binding duties; guidance and existing regulators fill the gap.

⚠ Status as of 7 November 2026. AI law is changing quickly; Colorado rewrote its act before it took effect, and the EU moved its own deadlines in 2026. Before citing any date or obligation in assessed work, check the current official text listed in the references.

Key framework 2: Sources of Harm in the ML Lifecycle

Law tells you who is accountable; this taxonomy tells you where harm actually enters a system. It locates distinct sources of harm at specific points in the machine learning lifecycle, from historical bias in the world to deployment and evaluation bias.

Key Points

  • Historical, representation, measurement, aggregation bias
  • Learning, evaluation and deployment bias downstream
  • Each source needs a different remedy
  • 'Debiasing the data' addresses only one of seven

Prepare for the Business Challenge

Aurora Health Group operates across Europe. Before the session, think through these questions for any one of its four proposed systems:

  1. Is it an "AI system" under Article 3(1)? Is Aurora its provider or its deployer, and could that change if Aurora adapts the system?
  2. Where does it sit in the pyramid, and from which date do its obligations apply?
  3. If the vendor were based in the United States or South Korea, which rules would apply to the vendor, and which to Aurora?
