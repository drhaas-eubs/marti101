Unit 1 · Presentation

Digital Transformation &amp; Artificial Intelligence

Digital transformation fails as a technology project and succeeds as a leadership decision about where value moves.

1.1 Digital Transformation

The use of technology to radically improve performance or reach of an enterprise

Digital transformation is the use of digital technology to radically improve the performance or reach of an enterprise, achieved by combining investment in technology (digital intensity) with the leadership capability required to drive change through the organisation (transformation-management intensity). It is distinguished from technology deployment by the requirement that operating model, customer experience and business processes are redesigned, not merely automated (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014; Vial, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.2 Artificial Intelligence

Machine-based systems that infer, from input, how to generate outputs that influence environments

An artificial intelligence system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments; different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment (OECD, 2024; Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 3(1)).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.3 Digital Maturity

The degree to which a firm has aligned technology, capability and governance to a digital operating model

Digital maturity is the extent to which an organisation has developed and aligned the technological assets, human capabilities, processes and governance mechanisms required to compete through digital means. It is expressed as a position on an ordered scale of stages rather than as a binary state, and it is measured across multiple dimensions simultaneously so that unevenness between them becomes visible (Kane et al., 2017; Chanias and Hess, 2016).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.4 Digitisation and Digitalisation

Converting analogue to digital, versus changing how work is done with digital means

Digitisation is the technical conversion of analogue information into a digital format, leaving the underlying process unchanged. Digitalisation is the use of digital technologies to change a business process, business model or the way value is created and captured. The terms are not synonymous and not interchangeable, and the distinction between them separates cost-reduction projects from transformation (Gartner, 2019; Verhoef et al., 2021).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.5 Business Model Innovation

Deliberate change to how a firm creates, delivers and captures value

Business model innovation is a deliberate and non-trivial change to one or more elements of the system by which a firm creates value for customers, delivers that value, and captures a share of it as revenue and margin. It is distinguished from product innovation because the object changed is the architecture of the business rather than the offering itself (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010; Amit and Zott, 2012).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.6 Disruptive Innovation

A process by which a low-end or new-market entrant displaces established competitors

Disruptive innovation is a process by which a product or service initially takes root in simple applications at the low end of a market, or creates a new market among non-consumers, and then moves upmarket over time until it displaces established competitors. The term describes the trajectory of an entrant relative to incumbent performance, not the novelty or impressiveness of the technology itself (Christensen, 1997; Christensen, Raynor and McDonald, 2015).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.7 Technology Adoption Lifecycle

The sequence of adopter groups through which an innovation diffuses

The technology adoption lifecycle is the sequence of distinct adopter categories - innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards - through which an innovation diffuses over time, distributed approximately normally across a population. Each category adopts for different reasons and responds to different evidence, so a message that persuades one will not persuade the next (Rogers, 1962; Moore, 1991).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 1 glossary
1.8 Value Migration

The flow of economic value away from obsolete business designs toward designs that better serve priorities

Value migration is the movement of economic value away from business designs that no longer meet customer priorities and toward designs that do, observable as a sustained shift in market value relative to revenue across the competitors in an industry. The unit of analysis is the business design rather than the firm or the product, and migration can occur while the losing firm's revenue is still growing (Slywotzky, 1996).

Unit 2 · Role Play

Business Leadership in the AI Era

AI literacy is not knowing how models work; it is knowing which question to ask second.

2.1 AI Literacy

The competence to use, interpret, evaluate and question AI systems in a working context

AI literacy is the set of competencies that enables a person to use artificial intelligence systems knowledgeably, to interpret and evaluate their outputs, to communicate about them accurately, and to judge when a system should not be relied upon. Since 2 February 2025 it carries a legal meaning in the European Union: Article 4 of the AI Act requires providers and deployers to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff operating AI systems on their behalf (Long and Magerko, 2020; Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 4).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.2 Psychological Safety

A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking

Psychological safety is a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking: that speaking up with a question, a concern, a mistake or a dissenting view will not lead to embarrassment, rejection or punishment. It is a property of the group climate rather than of individual personality, and it is distinct from trust, which concerns a dyad, and from cohesion, which can suppress dissent (Edmondson, 1999).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.3 Upper Echelons Theory

Organisational outcomes are partly predicted by the characteristics of the top management team

Upper echelons theory holds that organisational outcomes - strategic choices and performance levels - are partially predicted by the observable and psychological characteristics of the dominant coalition of senior managers, because executives act on their personalised interpretations of the situations they face, and those interpretations are shaped by their experience, values and cognitive base (Hambrick and Mason, 1984; Hambrick, 2007).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.4 Organisational Culture

The pattern of shared basic assumptions a group has learned and teaches to new members

Organisational culture is the pattern of shared basic assumptions that a group learned as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, which has worked well enough to be considered valid and is therefore taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think and feel in relation to those problems. It operates on three levels: visible artefacts, espoused values, and the underlying assumptions that actually govern behaviour (Schein, 1985).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.5 Change Leadership

The work of establishing direction and mobilising people through a transition

Change leadership is the work of establishing direction, aligning people to it and motivating them through a transition, as distinct from change management, which is the work of planning, resourcing, sequencing and controlling that transition. Leadership addresses the human transition that follows an organisational change; management addresses the change itself, and the two are complementary rather than interchangeable (Kotter, 1996; Bridges, 1991).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.6 Human-in-the-Loop

A control design in which a human decision is required within the system's operating cycle

Human-in-the-loop is a control design in which a human being is positioned inside the operating cycle of an automated system so that the system cannot complete a defined action without human input, review or approval. It is distinguished from human-on-the-loop, where the human supervises and may intervene but the system proceeds by default, and from human-in-command, where humans retain authority over whether the system operates at all (Amershi et al., 2014; HLEG AI, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.7 Algorithm Aversion

The tendency to discard an algorithm after seeing it err, even when it outperforms the human

Algorithm aversion is the tendency of people to lose confidence in an algorithmic forecaster more quickly than in a human forecaster after seeing each make the same mistake, and consequently to prefer the human even when the algorithm is demonstrably more accurate. Its counterpart, algorithm appreciation, is the tendency to over-rely on algorithmic advice in low-stakes or unfamiliar tasks; both are errors of calibration rather than of preference (Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, 2015; Logg, Minson and Moore, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 2 glossary
2.8 Decision Rights

The formal allocation of who may decide, who must be consulted, and who is accountable

Decision rights are the formal allocation across roles of the authority to make a specified class of decision, the obligation to provide input to it, and the accountability for its consequences. Allocating them requires answering four questions for each decision: what is being decided, who decides, how the decision is made, and when it is made. Ambiguity in this allocation, rather than in the analysis, is the common cause of decisions that are made repeatedly and never implemented (Rogers and Blenko, 2006).

Unit 3 · Case Study

Business Strategies in the AI Era

An AI strategy that does not say what the firm will stop doing is a budget with a vocabulary problem.

3.1 AI Strategy

A coherent set of choices about where AI will be applied and what will not be done

An AI strategy is a coherent set of integrated choices specifying where in the business artificial intelligence will be applied, what capability will be built or acquired to apply it, how the resulting advantage will be defended, and - definitionally - what the firm will therefore stop doing. A list of intended applications without the corresponding renunciations is a budget, not a strategy, because it makes no choice (Rumelt, 2011; Iansiti and Lakhani, 2020).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.2 Data Strategy

The choices governing how data is sourced, governed and used to serve the business strategy

A data strategy is the set of choices governing how an organisation sources, stores, governs, shares and exploits data in service of its business strategy. A central choice is the balance between defensive objectives - security, integrity, regulatory compliance - and offensive objectives - analytics, customer insight, competitive differentiation - since the architectures that optimise for one impede the other (DalleMule and Davenport, 2017).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.3 Competitive Advantage

Superior value creation that rivals cannot readily replicate or substitute

A competitive advantage exists where a firm creates greater economic value than its rivals and the source of that value is not simultaneously being exploited by them. It is sustained where the underlying resource is valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and organisationally exploitable - the VRIO conditions - and it is competitive parity, not advantage, where a resource is valuable but widely available (Barney, 1991; Porter, 1985).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.4 AI Operating Model

How AI capability, decision rights and delivery are organised across centre and business

An AI operating model is the arrangement of people, decision rights, processes, platform and governance through which an organisation converts AI capability into operating results. Its principal design choice is the distribution of capability between a central function and the business units - centralised, federated with a centre of excellence, or fully devolved - and each option trades delivery speed against consistency, reuse and control (Fountaine, McCarthy and Saleh, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.5 Build-Buy-Partner Decision

Choosing whether to develop internally, procure, or ally for a required capability

The build-buy-partner decision is the choice of governance mode for acquiring a required capability: internal development, market procurement, or a collaborative arrangement such as an alliance or joint venture. In transaction cost economics the choice turns on asset specificity, uncertainty and the frequency of the transaction, with internalisation preferred as specificity and uncertainty rise, because market contracting then becomes vulnerable to opportunism (Williamson, 1985; Capron and Mitchell, 2012).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.6 Scalable AI Business Model

A model whose marginal cost of serving an additional user does not rise with volume

A scalable AI business model is one in which the cost of serving an additional customer or processing an additional transaction does not rise in proportion to volume, so that value delivered grows faster than the resources consumed. Iansiti and Lakhani identify three properties that distinguish such models - the absence of operational diseconomies of scale, positive returns to scope from reusing data across domains, and continuous learning effects - and locate the constraint on all three in the operating model rather than the algorithm (Iansiti and Lakhani, 2020).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.7 Profit Pool

The total profit earned across all activities in a value chain, mapped by segment

A profit pool is the total profit earned at all points along the value chain of an industry, disaggregated by activity or segment and displayed against the revenue share of each, so that the concentration of profit becomes visible independently of the concentration of revenue. Profit pools are frequently distributed very differently from revenue pools, and they reallocate over time as technology alters which activity is scarce (Gadiesh and Gilbert, 1998).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 3 glossary
3.8 AI Portfolio Management

Allocating scarce delivery capacity across AI initiatives by risk and time horizon

AI portfolio management is the allocation of an organisation's scarce data, engineering and change-management capacity across a set of AI initiatives that differ in risk, time to value and strategic horizon. It requires that initiatives be balanced across horizons - core improvement, adjacent extension and transformational bets - and that the constraint being allocated be delivery capacity rather than capital, since capital is rarely the binding limit (Baghai, Coley and White, 1999; Nagji and Tuff, 2012).

Unit 4 · Mini-Hackathon

Leading Organisational AI Adoption

Adoption is not the last phase of an AI project; it is the only phase in which value is created.

4.1 AI Adoption

The process by which an approved AI system becomes routine practice in the operating business

AI adoption is the process by which an approved artificial intelligence system passes from availability into routine use in the operating business, such that the work is genuinely performed differently and the intended benefit is realised. Adoption is distinguished from deployment, which is a technical event, and from acceptance, which is an attitude; only adoption is observable in changed behaviour and changed outcomes (Rogers, 2003; Fichman, 2000).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.2 AI Roadmap

A time-phased plan linking AI initiatives to capability, dependency and business outcome

An AI roadmap is a time-phased plan that links a sequence of AI initiatives to the capabilities each requires, the dependencies between them, and the business outcomes each is intended to produce. It is a coordination instrument rather than a schedule: its function is to make visible which capability must exist before which initiative can proceed, so that sequencing errors are detected in planning rather than in delivery (Phaal, Farrukh and Probert, 2004).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.3 Proof of Concept and Pilot Purgatory

A bounded feasibility test, and the failure state in which pilots never reach production

A proof of concept is a small, time-boxed exercise designed to establish whether a proposed approach is technically feasible, and it is complete when that question is answered either way. Pilot purgatory is the organisational failure state in which a portfolio of such exercises accumulates, each judged successful on its own terms, while none reaches production - because the criterion applied was feasibility rather than scalability, and no exit decision was ever required (World Economic Forum and McKinsey, 2018).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.4 Change Management

The structured approach to moving individuals and organisations to a future state

Change management is the structured approach by which individuals, teams and organisations are moved from a current state to a defined future state so that an intended benefit is realised. Where change leadership addresses direction and motivation, change management addresses the planning, sequencing, resourcing, communication and reinforcement of the transition, and it operates on the individual as the unit of change (Hiatt, 2006; Kotter, 1996).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.5 MLOps

The engineering discipline for reliably deploying, monitoring and maintaining models in production

MLOps is the set of engineering practices for reliably deploying, monitoring, retraining and retiring machine-learning models in production. It extends DevOps by adding the two dependencies that distinguish machine-learning systems from conventional software: the behaviour of the system depends on data as well as on code, and the system degrades over time as the world it was trained on changes, without any change to the code (Sculley et al., 2015; Kreuzberger, Kuhl and Hirschl, 2023).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.6 Reskilling and Upskilling

Training for a different role, and deepening capability within the current one

Upskilling is the development of additional capability within a person's existing role so that the role can be performed to a changed standard. Reskilling is the development of the capability required for a materially different role, undertaken because the original role has been substantially altered or eliminated. The distinction matters because the two have different costs, durations, success rates and organisational obligations (World Economic Forum, 2023; Illanes et al., 2018).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.7 Technology Acceptance Model

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict intention to use a system

The Technology Acceptance Model holds that an individual's intention to use an information system is determined principally by two beliefs: perceived usefulness, the degree to which the person believes the system will enhance their job performance, and perceived ease of use, the degree to which they believe using it will be free of effort. Perceived ease of use also influences perceived usefulness, and usefulness is the stronger predictor of the two (Davis, 1989).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 4 glossary
4.8 Benefits Realisation

The management process ensuring that promised benefits are defined, owned and delivered

Benefits realisation management is the process of identifying, defining, planning, tracking and reviewing the benefits attributed to an investment, so that they are actually delivered rather than merely forecast. Each benefit must have a named owner outside the delivery team, a baseline measurement taken before the change, and a defined measurement point after it; benefits without all three are unverifiable claims (Ward and Daniel, 2012; Bradley, 2010).

Unit 5 · Debate

AI's Ripple Effects: Business, Economy &amp; Environment

The effects of AI that a firm can measure are rarely the effects that decide whether it was worth doing.

5.1 Productivity Paradox

Measured productivity gains lag the deployment of a transformative technology

The productivity paradox is the observed discrepancy between rapid deployment of a powerful information technology and the absence of a corresponding rise in measured aggregate productivity. The modern explanation is that general purpose technologies require large, slow and unmeasured investments in complementary intangible assets - process redesign, skills, organisational capital - which depress measured productivity while they are being made and raise it only afterwards (Solow, 1987; Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson, 2021).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.2 General Purpose Technology

A pervasive technology that improves over time and spawns complementary innovation

A general purpose technology is a technology characterised by three properties: pervasiveness across many sectors, continuing improvement over time that lowers the cost of use, and the capacity to spawn complementary innovation in the sectors that adopt it. Steam power, electricity and the semiconductor are the canonical cases; the economic significance of such technologies lies less in their direct output than in the innovation they enable elsewhere (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg, 1995).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.3 Skill-Biased Technological Change

Technical change that raises demand for some skills and lowers it for others

Skill-biased technological change is technical change that shifts relative demand across skill groups, raising the productivity and therefore the wages of favoured groups relative to others. The refined task-based account holds that the bias operates on tasks rather than on skill levels: technologies substitute for routine tasks - whether manual or cognitive - and complement non-routine tasks, which produces polarisation rather than a simple skill gradient (Autor, Levy and Murnane, 2003; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.4 Labour Market Displacement

Reduction in labour demand for tasks a technology performs, net of reinstatement

Labour market displacement is the reduction in demand for labour in tasks that a technology has come to perform. Its net employment effect depends on the balance between the displacement effect, which lowers labour demand at given output, and countervailing effects - the productivity effect, capital accumulation, and above all the reinstatement effect, in which new tasks are created in which labour holds comparative advantage (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.5 Externality

A cost or benefit borne by a third party who is not party to the transaction

An externality is a cost or benefit arising from an economic activity that falls on a party who neither chose to incur it nor is compensated for it, and which therefore does not appear in the price of the activity. Because the decision-maker does not bear the full cost, activity generating negative externalities is over-supplied relative to the socially efficient level; correction requires that the external cost be internalised through regulation, taxation or assignment of rights (Pigou, 1920; Coase, 1960).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.6 Green AI

Treating computational and environmental cost as a reported outcome alongside accuracy

Green AI is the practice of treating the computational and environmental cost of machine-learning research and deployment as a first-class evaluation criterion alongside accuracy, and of reporting it. It is contrasted with Red AI, in which accuracy improvements are pursued through increased computation without that cost being reported. The proposed reporting measure is total floating-point operations required to produce a result, which is hardware-independent and comparable across studies (Schwartz et al., 2020).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.7 Life Cycle Assessment

Standardised accounting of environmental impact across a product's whole life

Life cycle assessment is the compilation and evaluation of the inputs, outputs and potential environmental impacts of a product system throughout its life cycle, from raw material extraction through production, use and end-of-life. It is standardised in four phases - goal and scope definition, inventory analysis, impact assessment, and interpretation - by ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, and its results are only comparable where the system boundary and functional unit are identically defined (ISO, 2006).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 5 glossary
5.8 Digital Divide

Systematic inequality in access to, use of, and benefit from digital technology

The digital divide is the systematic inequality between individuals, groups, regions or states in access to digital technology, in the skills and autonomy to use it effectively, and in the outcomes obtained from that use. The literature distinguishes three levels: the first-level divide in physical access, the second-level divide in skills and usage, and the third-level divide in the tangible benefits realised (van Dijk, 2005; Scheerder, van Deursen and van Dijk, 2017).

Unit 6 · Business Challenge · Capstone

Human-Centred Implications of AI: Ethics, Society &amp; Regulation

Risk is not a property of the technology; it is a property of the decision to deploy it, and it belongs to someone.

6.1 AI Ethics

The applied field concerned with the moral acceptability of designing and deploying AI

AI ethics is the field of applied ethics concerned with the moral acceptability of the design, development, deployment and use of artificial intelligence systems, and with the obligations of those who commission and operate them. In the European context it is operationalised through the four principles of the Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI - respect for human autonomy, prevention of harm, fairness and explicability - and their seven implementing requirements (HLEG AI, 2019; Floridi and Cowls, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.2 EU AI Act

The European Union's horizontal, risk-tiered regulation of artificial intelligence systems

The EU AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, a horizontal regulation applying to any AI system placed on the Union market or whose output is used in the Union, regardless of the provider's place of establishment. It assigns obligations according to four risk tiers - unacceptable, high, limited and minimal - with separate obligations for general-purpose AI models, and it distinguishes the duties of providers from those of deployers (European Parliament and Council, 2024).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.3 Algorithmic Bias

Systematic and unfair discrimination in the outputs of an algorithmic system

Algorithmic bias is systematic and repeatable error in the output of a computational system that creates unfair outcomes, such as privileging one group of users over another. It arises from pre-existing bias embedded in the training data or in social practice, technical bias arising from the design of the system, and emergent bias arising when a system is used in a context different from the one for which it was built (Friedman and Nissenbaum, 1996; Mehrabi et al., 2021).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.4 Explainability

The property of a system whose outputs can be accounted for in humanly intelligible terms

Explainability is the property of an AI system whose behaviour and specific outputs can be accounted for in terms intelligible to a relevant human audience. It is distinguished from interpretability, which concerns whether the model's internal mechanism is directly inspectable, and it is audience-relative: the explanation adequate for a data scientist differs from that required by a decision subject or a regulator (Guidotti et al., 2018; Rudin, 2019).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.5 AI Governance

The structures and processes by which an organisation directs and controls its AI activity

AI governance is the system of structures, decision rights, policies, processes and controls by which an organisation directs its AI activity and holds itself accountable for the outcomes. In the standardised form it comprises four functions - govern, map, measure and manage - of which govern is a culture and accountability function running across the other three rather than a preliminary stage (NIST, 2023; ISO/IEC, 2023).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.6 Accountability Gap

The condition in which harm occurs and no party can properly be held responsible

The accountability gap - also termed the responsibility gap - is the condition in which an autonomous or learning system causes harm and no human party satisfies the conditions ordinarily required for moral or legal responsibility, because no one had the control or the foreseeability that responsibility presupposes. Many-hands problems in distributed development compound it, as responsibility diffuses across designers, deployers, operators and data providers (Matthias, 2004; Santoni de Sio and Mecacci, 2021).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.7 Data Protection by Design

Embedding data protection into processing from the outset and by default

Data protection by design and by default is the legal obligation, under Article 25 of the General Data Protection Regulation, to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures that give effect to the data-protection principles at the time processing means are determined and during processing itself, and to ensure that by default only personal data necessary for each specific purpose is processed. It converts a design philosophy into a binding duty on the controller (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 25; Cavoukian, 2009).

Reference sheet (PDF) Unit 6 glossary
6.8 Trustworthy AI

AI that is lawful, ethical and technically robust across its whole lifecycle

Trustworthy AI is artificial intelligence that satisfies three components throughout its lifecycle: it is lawful, complying with applicable law; ethical, respecting ethical principles and values; and robust, both technically and socially, since even well-intentioned systems can cause unintentional harm. The three components are necessary and not individually sufficient, and the framework is operationalised through seven requirements with an accompanying assessment list (HLEG AI, 2019).
