Westerman Digital Maturity Matrix

Two axes of maturity — digital intensity and transformation-management intensity

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 1 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Digital Maturity Matrix classifies firms on two independent axes — digital intensity (investment in technology-enabled initiatives) and transformation-management intensity (the leadership capabilities that drive change) — producing four maturity types: Beginners, Conservatives, Fashionistas and Digirati. Digirati, strong on both axes, consistently outperform peers on financial measures (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Developed by George Westerman (MIT), Didier Bonnet (Capgemini) and Andrew McAfee (MIT) from a multi-year study of more than 400 large firms conducted jointly by the MIT Center for Digital Business and Capgemini Consulting (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

The authors observed that many firms invested heavily in digital technology yet saw little return, while others with modest spend pulled ahead. They concluded that technology intensity alone does not explain success — the missing variable is the managerial capability to lead change — and built the matrix to make that second dimension visible and measurable.

FIGURE 1 · WESTERMAN DIGITAL MATURITY MATRIX

Figure 1. Four maturity types from digital intensity × management intensity (Westerman, Bonnet & McAfee).

HOW IT IS USED

Score the firm on digital intensity and on transformation-management intensity, then place it in one of the four quadrants and read the implied priorities.

WHEN IT IS USED

At the diagnostic phase of a transformation programme, and periodically thereafter to track movement toward the Digirati quadrant.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Executive teams, transformation officers and consultants benchmarking digital maturity.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Where earlier maturity models treated digital progress as a single technology ladder, the matrix separates capability from investment, showing that management intensity — not spend — distinguishes the leaders (Westerman, Bonnet and McAfee, 2014).

Rogers' Five Domains of Digital Transformation

Rethinking strategy across customers, competition, data, innovation and value

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 2 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Rogers' Five Domains framework holds that digital transformation is fundamentally a matter of strategy, not technology, and that it requires rethinking five domains of the business: customers, competition, data, innovation and value. In each domain, digital forces overturn long-held assumptions and demand new strategic rules (Rogers, 2016).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Developed by David L. Rogers, faculty member at Columbia Business School, in The Digital Transformation Playbook (2016), drawing on his executive teaching and case research on incumbent firms adapting to digital competition.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Rogers argued that established companies too often frame digital transformation as an IT upgrade, missing the deeper shifts in how value is created and captured. The five domains give incumbents a strategic map for change rather than a technology checklist.

FIGURE 1 · ROGERS' FIVE DOMAINS OF DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION

Figure 1. Digital transformation is a strategic rethink across five domains (David L. Rogers).

HOW IT IS USED

Work through each of the five domains, challenge the pre-digital assumption in each, and adopt the corresponding new strategic rule.

WHEN IT IS USED

When an established firm must reframe its strategy for a digital and AI-driven competitive environment.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Strategy leaders and executives in incumbent organisations undergoing transformation.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Compared with technology-centric maturity models, Rogers' framework is explicitly strategic, reframing transformation as a rethinking of competitive rules rather than an adoption of tools (Rogers, 2016).

Venkatraman Reconfiguration Levels

Five levels of IT-enabled transformation — from automation to business-scope redefinition

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 3 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Venkatraman's model describes five levels of IT-enabled business transformation of rising potential benefit and rising organisational change: localised exploitation, internal integration, business process redesign, business network redesign, and business scope redefinition. The first two are evolutionary; the upper three are revolutionary and require deliberate management (Venkatraman, 1994).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Developed by N. Venkatraman, then at MIT's Sloan School and later the David J. McGrath Professor at Boston University's Questrom School, in a 1994 Sloan Management Review article grounded in studies of large corporations including IBM and Bose (Venkatraman, 1994).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Venkatraman entered the 1990s sceptical that IT investment was paying off, noting disappointing productivity gains. He argued the benefits depend on the degree of business transformation undertaken, and built the levels to help managers match ambition to the change their organisation could absorb.

FIGURE 1 · VENKATRAMAN RECONFIGURATION LEVELS

Figure 1. Localised exploitation up to business-scope redefinition (N. Venkatraman).

HOW IT IS USED

Identify the firm's current level, then decide how far up the ladder to move — matching the potential benefit of higher levels against the organisational change and risk they demand.

WHEN IT IS USED

When scoping the ambition of an IT- or AI-enabled initiative, and when sequencing from integration toward redefinition.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

CIOs, transformation leaders and strategists calibrating the scale of change.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Against the prevailing view that IT value came automatically from adoption, Venkatraman insisted the benefits scale only with the degree of business transformation — distinguishing automation from genuine reconfiguration (Venkatraman, 1994).

Rewired Six Building Blocks

The six capabilities a digital-and-AI transformation must build together

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 4 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The Rewired framework identifies six capabilities an enterprise must develop in concert to succeed at digital and AI transformation: a clear strategy, digital talent, a product-and-platform operating model, a distributed technology environment, trusted and consumable data, and disciplined adoption to capture value. The central claim is that these must move together — strength in one cannot compensate for weakness in another (Lamarre, Smaje and Zemmel, 2023).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Developed by Eric Lamarre, Kate Smaje and Rodney Zemmel, senior leaders at McKinsey & Company, in Rewired (2023), distilling patterns observed across hundreds of client transformations.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

The authors found that most transformations underdeliver because organisations invest in isolated pieces — a data platform, a talent programme — without the surrounding capabilities to convert them into value. The six building blocks provide an integrated operating blueprint aimed squarely at value capture.

FIGURE 1 · REWIRED SIX BUILDING BLOCKS

Figure 1. Strategy, talent, operating model, technology, data, adoption (Lamarre, Smaje & Zemmel).

HOW IT IS USED

Assess maturity across all six building blocks, then invest to raise them together rather than sequentially, with explicit measurement of value captured.

WHEN IT IS USED

When planning or course-correcting an enterprise-wide digital or AI transformation.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Transformation sponsors, CDOs and executive teams accountable for value delivery.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Where earlier playbooks emphasised technology or strategy in isolation, Rewired's contribution is the insistence on simultaneity and on measured value capture as the test of success (Lamarre, Smaje and Zemmel, 2023).

Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

An eight-step process for leading organisational change

SLIBRARY 01 · SHEET 5 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

Kotter's 8-Step Change Model sets out a sequential process for leading large-scale organisational change: establish a sense of urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a strategic vision, enlist a volunteer army, enable action by removing barriers, generate short-term wins, sustain acceleration, and institute change in the culture (Kotter, 1996).

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Developed by John P. Kotter, Konosuke Matsushita Professor of Leadership Emeritus at Harvard Business School, first in a 1995 Harvard Business Review article and then in Leading Change (1996), based on his study of why corporate change efforts fail.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Kotter observed that roughly seventy per cent of change initiatives failed, most often because leaders declared victory too early or neglected the human side of change. The eight steps codify the sequence he found in successful transformations, with particular weight on urgency and short-term wins.

FIGURE 1 · KOTTER'S 8-STEP CHANGE MODEL

Figure 1. Urgency → coalition → vision → … → anchoring (John P. Kotter).

HOW IT IS USED

Move through the eight steps in order, resisting the temptation to skip urgency-building or to declare success before the change is anchored in the culture.

WHEN IT IS USED

When leading any significant organisational change, including AI-driven transformation of roles and processes.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Change leaders, sponsors and managers at every level guiding people through transition.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Against ad-hoc change management, Kotter offered a repeatable, sequenced process and identified premature victory declarations as the characteristic failure mode (Kotter, 1996).

AI–ML–DL Nested Circles

What sits inside what, and why the distinction matters

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 1 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A conceptual map showing artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning as nested sets: deep learning is a subset of machine learning, which is itself a subset of the broader field of artificial intelligence. The nesting clarifies that not all AI is machine learning, and not all machine learning is deep learning.

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Popularised in the deep-learning research community and codified in the standard graduate textbook by Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio and Aaron Courville (2016).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

Business and policy discussion routinely conflates AI, ML and deep learning, producing muddled expectations. The nesting was offered to give non-specialists a precise vocabulary for what a given system actually is.

FIGURE 1 · AI–ML–DL NESTED CIRCLES

Figure 1. What sits inside what, and why the distinction matters (Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville).

HOW IT IS USED

Place any AI claim in the correct nested layer before evaluating it, distinguishing rule-based AI from learned systems.

WHEN IT IS USED

At the start of any AI-literacy effort, and whenever a vendor claim needs classifying.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Executives, analysts and students building foundational AI literacy.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Where earlier definitions treated AI as one undifferentiated field, the nested view separates symbolic AI from statistical learning and from deep neural methods.

Moravec's Paradox

Hard for humans is easy for machines — and vice versa

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 4 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

The observation that tasks humans find hard — abstract reasoning, calculation — are comparatively easy for computers, while tasks humans find effortless — perception, movement, dexterity — are extremely hard to automate.

WHO DEVELOPED IT

Articulated by roboticist Hans Moravec, with related observations by Marvin Minsky and Rodney Brooks, in the late 1980s (Moravec, 1988).

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

To correct the naive assumption that intelligence is a single scale and that machines would master human skills in the order humans find them difficult.

FIGURE 1 · MORAVEC'S PARADOX

Figure 1. Hard for humans is easy for machines — and vice versa (Hans Moravec).

HOW IT IS USED

Use it to set realistic expectations: expect strength in computation and pattern-matching, weakness in physical and sensorimotor tasks.

WHEN IT IS USED

When forecasting which activities AI and robotics will and will not soon perform.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Strategists, roboticists and anyone setting AI expectations.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Overturned the intuition that 'harder for humans' means 'harder for machines', reframing the difficulty landscape.

Stochastic Parrots — Model Limits

Fluency above the waterline, no understanding below

SLIBRARY 02 · SHEET 5 / 5
PROFESSIONAL DEFINITION

A critical framing of large language models as systems that stitch together linguistic form from statistical patterns without access to meaning, carrying risks of bias, opacity, environmental cost and the illusion of understanding.

WHO DEVELOPED IT

From the paper by Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell (writing as 'Shmargaret Shmitchell'), presented at FAccT 2021.

WHY IT WAS DEVELOPED

To counter uncritical enthusiasm for ever-larger language models by naming their limits and social costs.

FIGURE 1 · STOCHASTIC PARROTS — MODEL LIMITS

Figure 1. Fluency above the waterline, no understanding below (Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major et al.).

HOW IT IS USED

Use the critique to interrogate any large-model deployment for bias, provenance, cost and the gap between fluency and understanding.

WHEN IT IS USED

When evaluating generative-AI systems and their risks.

BY WHOM IT IS USED

Ethics reviewers, risk managers and critical practitioners.

INNOVATION RELATIVE TO THE PREDECESSOR TOOL

Named and popularised a rigorous critical vocabulary for the limits of scale in language models.
